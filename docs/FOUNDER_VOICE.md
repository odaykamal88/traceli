# Odey founder voice guide

Updated: 28 August 2026

This guide governs founder-bylined Traceli articles, posts, interviews and email briefs.

## Public identity

**Byline:** Odey, Founder of Traceli

**Short biography:**

> Odey is the founder of Traceli. He previously worked across product and operations at the United Nations, Google and Careem. Those environments showed him how consequential decisions depend on evidence spread across policies, systems, files and people. He is building Traceli so the teams responsible for donor-funded procurement can make the proof behind an award clear before it is signed.

Do not add titles, reporting lines, achievements, project names, tenure, locations or responsibilities that Odey has not confirmed. The organizations above are personal professional history. They are not Traceli customers, partners or endorsements.

## Voice

Write in Odey's first person when the piece carries his byline.

The voice should feel like an experienced operator explaining something he has seen clearly. It is direct, specific and calm. It respects procurement professionals and treats accountability as serious operational work. It does not sound like a consultant, campaign copywriter or generic technology founder.

Prefer:

- a concrete observation before an abstract claim;
- ordinary words and short, complete sentences;
- one strong point per paragraph;
- specific institutional consequences and exact source status;
- earned conviction rather than theatrical certainty;
- a natural mixture of short and medium-length sentences;
- quiet first-person context when Odey's experience genuinely explains his view;
- a practical question the reader can apply to one real award.

Avoid:

- inflated claims, moral judgment and fear theatre;
- generic openings about a fast-changing world or a complex landscape;
- phrases such as `delve`, `unlock`, `game changer`, `at its core`, `now more than ever`, `this is not just`, `the reality is`, `here is the thing` and `in today's world`;
- repetitive contrasts such as `not X, but Y`;
- manufactured groups of three used only for rhythm;
- a heading every few paragraphs;
- single-sentence paragraphs repeated for dramatic effect;
- fake quotations, composite anecdotes presented as real, and invented detail;
- an instruction to `book a demo` after every insight.

## Punctuation and formatting

- Do not use semicolons in public copy.
- Do not use em dashes or spaced hyphens as sentence punctuation. End the sentence or use a comma.
- Keep necessary compound terms such as `donor-funded`, `source-linked`, `pre-award` and `six-week` when they improve clarity.
- Use one space after punctuation and no trailing whitespace.
- Keep paragraph spacing consistent. Do not create artificial white space to make ordinary sentences look profound.
- Use bullets only for an actual checklist, sequence or set of distinct facts.
- Use bold sparingly. The writing should carry the emphasis.
- Avoid excessive parentheses, colons and rhetorical questions.

## Personal experience

Odey's tender stories can create valuable first-party evidence, but they must remain distinct from official public records and customer evidence.

Label each story internally as one of:

- **Private founder recollection:** useful for product insight, not cleared for publication.
- **Anonymized pattern:** publishable only when identifying details are removed and the account is not misleading.
- **Corroborated account:** supported by a document, public record or second participant and reviewed for confidentiality.
- **Attributable story:** the organization or individual has explicitly approved the wording and attribution.

Never use confidential supplier information, an active tender, protected personal data or details that allow a supposedly anonymous institution to be identified. A founder recollection can explain why Odey cares about the problem. It cannot establish how common the problem is or prove a financial impact without supporting evidence.

## Editorial test

Before approval, read the piece aloud and ask:

1. Does this sound like a person who has done the work?
2. Is every consequence stated as precisely as its source allows?
3. Is the institution treated fairly?
4. Is there one useful thought the reader can carry into the next award meeting?
5. Would Odey say these words in a room with a procurement director, donor and auditor present?

If the answer to the last question is no, rewrite it.
